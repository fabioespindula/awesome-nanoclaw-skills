# Scenario Patterns

Use this reference when the user asks about futures, second-order effects, or plausible worlds. The goal is not prediction. The goal is to make different futures thinkable and testable.

## Scenario Template

For each scenario, define:

- **Name**: memorable but not cute.
- **Core logic**: why this future happens.
- **What must be true**: assumptions required.
- **Behavior change**: who acts differently.
- **Second-order effects**: what changes after the first change.
- **Winners**: who gains leverage, money, status, distribution, speed, or resilience.
- **Losers**: who loses leverage, margins, access, status, or relevance.
- **Signposts**: what evidence would suggest this scenario is arriving.
- **Failure mode**: what could prevent it.

## Scenario Archetypes

### Compression

A workflow that used to require many steps, people, or tools becomes one step.

Watch for:

- fewer handoffs;
- collapsed interfaces;
- new generalist roles;
- old middle layers losing value.

### Delegation

Users stop operating tools directly and delegate intent to agents, brokers, or intermediaries.

Watch for:

- trust and permission layers;
- audit trails;
- liability questions;
- discovery moving away from human browsing.

### Embeddedness

A product category stops being bought directly and becomes embedded inside another workflow or platform.

Watch for:

- app categories becoming features;
- bundled pricing;
- procurement moving to a larger system;
- loss of direct customer relationship.

### Rebundling

After unbundling creates fragmentation, users pay for coordination, integration, or trust.

Watch for:

- orchestration layers;
- managed services;
- marketplaces becoming operating systems;
- new "single pane" buyer demand.

### Trust Bottleneck

Capability exists, but adoption is gated by trust, liability, certification, reputation, or governance.

Watch for:

- insurance products;
- certifications;
- audit logs;
- human-in-the-loop defaults;
- procurement language around risk.

### Regulation Catches Up

Institutions define, restrict, tax, certify, or mandate behavior.

Watch for:

- draft rules;
- lawsuits;
- enforcement;
- standard-setting;
- compliance tooling.

### Counter-Reaction

Users, workers, buyers, or regulators reject the obvious future and create a market for the opposite.

Watch for:

- authenticity markets;
- human-made labels;
- local-first tools;
- privacy-first behavior;
- unions or professional bodies adapting.

### Commoditization and Escape

The core capability becomes cheap, forcing value to move to data, distribution, brand, workflow ownership, compliance, or taste.

Watch for:

- price collapse;
- feature parity;
- open-source substitutes;
- increased importance of proprietary context.

## Second-Order Effect Prompts

After identifying a first-order change, ask:

- If this becomes cheap, what becomes scarce?
- If this becomes fast, what becomes the bottleneck?
- If everyone can do this, what stops mattering?
- Who gets new leverage over whom?
- What new fraud, spam, or abuse appears?
- What new status game appears?
- What becomes regulated?
- What new profession appears?
- What old profession becomes a review, orchestration, or trust role?
- What behavior becomes embarrassing or obsolete?

## Time Horizons

Use simple time horizons:

- **Now**: already happening in pockets.
- **1-2 years**: plausible if current incentives continue.
- **3-5 years**: requires adoption, trust, integration, or regulation to mature.
- **5+ years**: structural speculation; label clearly.

## Scenario Quality Bar

A good scenario is:

- plausible without being obvious;
- specific enough to test;
- linked to mechanisms;
- explicit about assumptions;
- useful even if it turns out wrong.

A weak scenario is:

- a slogan;
- a single trend extrapolated forever;
- pure optimism or doom;
- dependent on "people will want better tools";
- missing winners, losers, and signposts.
