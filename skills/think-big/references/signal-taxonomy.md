# Signal Taxonomy

Use this reference when a `think-big` answer needs a sharper signal map. Do not load it for short answers unless the prompt depends on distinguishing durable change from noise.

## Strong Signals

Strong signals indicate that behavior, economics, institutions, or infrastructure may already be changing.

### Adoption Signals

- users repeat the behavior without being paid or prompted;
- teams budget for it;
- workflows change around it;
- people complain when it is removed;
- usage survives novelty decay;
- competitors copy the behavior;
- buyers create procurement language for it.

### Economic Signals

- costs fall by an order of magnitude;
- a bottleneck moves from production to distribution, trust, data, or compliance;
- margins shift across the value chain;
- pricing models change;
- budgets move from one category to another;
- buyers consolidate vendors;
- labor cost, coordination cost, or switching cost changes.

### Institutional Signals

- regulation names the category;
- courts or regulators force new behavior;
- standards bodies create language for it;
- compliance teams start asking about it;
- insurers, auditors, procurement teams, or schools adapt.

### Platform Signals

- major platforms expose APIs or restrict APIs;
- defaults change;
- distribution surfaces change;
- identity, payments, permissions, or reputation layers shift;
- app stores, search engines, social feeds, or agents change discovery.

### Labor and Org Signals

- new job titles appear;
- existing roles absorb new responsibilities;
- hiring requirements change;
- fewer people can produce more output;
- managers change review criteria;
- junior roles shrink, mutate, or become supervised by tools.

## Weak Signals

Weak signals are early, ambiguous, and easy to dismiss. They are useful when they point to a mechanism.

Look for:

- weird workarounds;
- persistent niche complaints;
- user behavior that looks "wrong" under the old model;
- subculture vocabulary;
- products that look too small but solve a sharp problem;
- edge cases becoming common;
- small teams doing work that used to require large teams;
- kids, hobbyists, or power users behaving differently from institutions;
- policy drafts or pre-regulatory language;
- new trust rituals;
- new forms of status.

## Noise Filters

Treat these as low-confidence unless supported by stronger evidence:

- viral demos without retention;
- funding announcements without customer evidence;
- founder threads claiming inevitability;
- consulting charts with unclear methodology;
- market maps that mostly sell the author's category;
- content engagement mistaken for adoption;
- "everyone is talking about it";
- one-off examples presented as structural change.

## Signal Questions

Ask:

- Who is changing behavior without being forced?
- Who is paying?
- What old workflow is becoming embarrassing, slow, expensive, or risky?
- What new default is forming?
- What hidden cost is collapsing?
- What bottleneck appears after the first bottleneck disappears?
- What complaint sounds minor today but could define the category later?

## Confidence Heuristic

- **High confidence**: multiple independent strong signals point to the same mechanism.
- **Medium confidence**: strong signals exist, but incentives or adoption depth are unclear.
- **Low confidence**: mostly weak signals, hype, or speculative extrapolation.

Use low-confidence signals, but label them.
